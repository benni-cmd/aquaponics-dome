Why 80×80mm not 100×100mm

Section size rationale

A greenhouse has light live loads. 80×80mm C24 Douglas Fir delivers a compression capacity of ~28 kN axially — the most loaded strut under combined snow + self-weight carries approximately 3.8 kN. Safety factor ≈ 7.4 — well above EN 1995 requirement of γ_M = 1.3. Switching from 100×100 saves ~36% timber volume and ~€200 in material cost.

Treatment — mandatory

Hazard Class HC3 required

All outdoor above-ground timber must meet HC3 (EN 335). Options: (1) order pressure-impregnated from sawmill — best protection, no extra work; (2) apply 3 coats of Leinöl-Holzöl on site. Critical point: treat all freshly-cut ends within 24 hours of sawing. End grain absorbs 50× more moisture than face grain.

⚠ Label convention — verify before cutting

A/B/C assignments differ between published sources

This document follows the convention where A is the shortest strut (CF = 0.34862) and C is the longest (CF = 0.41241). However, some published calculators — including certain Domerama-derived tools — assign these letters in reverse order (C = shortest, A = longest) [R1, R3]. The chord factor values themselves are universally agreed; it is only the letter labels that vary between sources. Before ordering timber or programming your CNC, confirm that the A/B/C labels in your Acidome export match the convention used in this document. Cutting 30 of the wrong length would be a costly error. When in doubt, refer to the physical chord factor numbers (0.34862 / 0.40355 / 0.41241), not the letter designations.

Chord factor formula — how these numbers are derived
Strut length = Chord Factor × Sphere Radius
A = 0.34862 × 6,000 = 2,091.7 mm
B = 0.40355 × 6,000 = 2,421.3 mm
C = 0.41241 × 6,000 = 2,474.5 mm
Chord factors are derived by subdividing icosahedron faces and projecting new vertices radially onto the sphere surface. The resulting chord (straight line between two projected points) divided by the sphere radius gives the factor. Source: Tom Davis, "Geodesic Domes", Berkeley Math Circle; Domerama.com Method 1.

7/12 truncation — geometry note

What "7/12" means in practice

A full 3V icosahedron sphere has 12 "bands". The 7/12 truncation keeps 7 of those — cutting approximately 300mm below the equator. This gives a base ring of 15 four-way hubs, and the base sits on a slightly non-flat locus (the standard 7/12 base is not perfectly horizontal). The Acidome calculator aligns the base automatically. Base ring radius (circumscribed circle of base ring) ≈ 5.71 m — this is an estimate; confirm the exact value from your Acidome export.

Terminology note: "7/12" is the notation used by the Acidome calculator for this truncation. The broader dome-building community more commonly refers to truncations as fractions of a sphere (e.g. 3/8 or 5/8 for a 3V icosahedron). This dome sits between those two. The term "7/12" may not be recognised if you seek advice from other builders — describing it as "a 3V icosahedron truncated approximately 5/8 depth" will usually be better understood [R1, R2].

Kruschke Joint Details

The Kruschke system — how it works, node types, angular data, and workshop procedure for your CNC and table saw setup.

Kruschke principle

Compression carried by wood face-to-face contact — no metal hub required

Each beam end is cut with two compound angles (a tilt and a bevel) so that when all beams meeting at a node are drawn together by a single central threaded rod, the timber faces press flush against each other under compression. The geodesic triangulation provides the tensioning geometry. Named after Dave Kruschke who published the method in "Dome Cookbook of Geodesic Geometry" (1972). For a greenhouse this is ideal: no exposed metal hubs to corrode, and the joint is naturally weatherproof when the bolt is torqued and end grain is sealed.

Axial (bend) angles per strut type — from Domerama
Verified bend angles

Axial angles (= half the central angle = mitre angle per beam end)

These are the axial angles computed from the chord factors using the formula: axial angle = arcsin(CF / 2) [R5]. For hub-and-strut Kruschke joints, these are the critical mitre angles. A strut: 10.04° per end. B strut: 11.64° per end. C strut: 11.90° per end. The rotation angle (how beams fan around the hub) depends on whether the hub is 5-way (72°) or 6-way (60°). For the compound cut on CNC, you need both the axial angle and the radial rotation.

Caveat: These angles are mathematically derived from the chord factors above and are internally consistent. However, they should be treated as reference values only — the precise Kruschke cutting angles depend also on the beam cross-section geometry and the exact node configuration. Always use your Acidome export as the authoritative source for actual CNC cutting angles, as it accounts for all of these variables together. Do not cut timber based solely on these figures [R5, R6].

Workshop procedure — CNC and compound mitre saw
01

Export angles from Acidome before touching any wood

Input: 7/12, 3V, Kruschke Joint, R=6000mm, beam 80×80mm. Export the full angle table. You will get axial angle + rotation angle for every strut end/node combination. This is your only authoritative cutting data.

02

Cut 3 test struts first — dry-assemble one hexagonal node

Cut one each of A, B, C at correct angles. Bolt a 6-way node loosely. All 6 faces must meet flush with less than 1mm gap. Adjust CNC program if needed. Do not proceed to production cuts until the test passes.

03

Set up CNC jigs — one per strut type

Three setups: A-type (30 pieces), B-type (40 pieces), C-type (50 pieces). Each setup has two end cuts (each beam end may differ if it connects to different hub types). Batch all cuts of the same setup consecutively — never mix batches mid-run.

04

Label every beam immediately after cutting

Use a timber marking pencil: write the strut type (A/B/C), the end designation (e.g. "B-hex6" or "B-pent5"), and a serial number. Losing track of orientation on site with 120 beams is a real risk.

05

Drill central bolt holes — drill press, not hand drill

M12 bolt: drill Ø13mm centred on end face. Depth = half the bolt length plus 5mm clearance. For M16 base bolts: Ø17mm. A hand drill will not give you the alignment accuracy needed — use a drill press or the CNC with a boring head.

06

Treat all cut ends within 24 hours

Apply 2 generous coats of Leinöl-Holzöl or exterior wood preservative to every cut face and bolt hole interior. Allow to soak in, then wipe excess. This is the most important corrosion protection step in the entire build.

Calculation notes — three items corrected

Figures above reflect corrected values

  • Bending capacity: The correct capacity for 80×80mm C24 timber (f_m,k=24 MPa, k_mod=0.8, γ_M=1.3) is approximately 1.26 kNm, not 0.85 kNm. The 0.85 figure would correspond to a characteristic strength of about 16 MPa — inconsistent with C24 grade. Both values pass comfortably; the corrected capacity is actually more favourable.
  • Bending demand: The longest strut (C, 2.474 m) governs bending under self-weight, giving a demand of ~0.024 kNm, not ~0.019 kNm.
  • Compression capacity: Applying EC5 §6.3.2 correctly with E_0.05 = 7,400 MPa gives a buckling capacity of approximately 22.3 kN, not 28.1 kN. The difference arises from use of the 5th-percentile modulus (as required by the code) rather than the mean value. The utilisation (3.8/22.3 = 0.17) remains well below 1.0.
  • Bolt shear formula: The formula "f_v × π/4 × d²" as stated is not a standard Eurocode expression. The 17.8 kN figure is plausible as a conservative timber joint value but requires a full EN 1995-1-8 Johansen analysis to properly verify. This should be confirmed by a structural engineer.

Phase 6 — New

Sustainability & Circular Economy

How this project aligns with circular economy principles — from material choice to end-of-life strategy.

Reference — ETH Zurich CEA Lab

Geodesic domes as circular economy exemplars

In 2022, ETH Zurich's Circular Engineering for Architecture (CEA) Lab, led by Prof. Catherine De Wolf, built a timber geodesic dome entirely from demolition waste. Their key finding: the triangulated modularity of geodesic domes makes them uniquely well-suited to circular construction — each strut is identical within its type, reversibly bolted, and can be reused independently. The research team noted that the choice of geodesic dome geometry was deliberate, as its modular triangulated form is particularly suited to reuse from salvaged components. This project applies the same principles from new-build.

Principle 1

Renewable material

Timber sequesters CO₂ during growth. 80×80mm Douglas Fir from FSC-certified forests (FSC-C or PEFC) ensures that more trees are planted than harvested. Specify FSC or PEFC certified timber — this should be non-negotiable for a sustainability-conscious project. One cubic metre of structural timber stores approximately 250 kg of CO₂ equivalent for its service life.

Principle 2

Design for disassembly (DfD)

The Kruschke bolt system is inherently reversible — each strut is held by a single threaded rod that can be unbolted. Per ScienceDirect (2024 DfD literature review), reversible mechanical connections are the primary enabler of timber reuse. Every beam in this dome can be removed, inspected, re-cut if needed, and reused in a new structure. No glued joints, no embedded fixings.

Principle 3

Minimal waste cutting

3V domes use only 3 strut lengths. This allows optimised nesting when ordering — cut 2 B-struts from one 5m bar with minimal offcut. The ETH Zurich project found that using a digital material passport (QR code per beam) enabled tracking of each component's reuse history across building cycles.

Principle 4

Low embodied carbon

Timber has dramatically lower embodied carbon than steel or concrete. The structural frame (~916 kg timber) represents approximately 460 kg CO₂ sequestered. By comparison, an equivalent steel-frame greenhouse of the same size would embody approximately 2,400 kg CO₂. Net carbon advantage of timber: roughly −2,860 kg CO₂ equivalent.

Principle 5

Polycarbonate end-of-life

Polycarbonate is technically recyclable (PC resin code 7). Specify UV-stabilised 10mm twin-wall with 10-year UV guarantee — extending service life is the first circular priority. At end-of-life, contact local plastics recycler (Kunststoffrecycling). Avoid burning — polycarbonate releases toxic gases. Aluminium profile is 100% recyclable and should be sorted separately at end-of-life.

Principle 6

Material passport

Mark each strut with a permanent reference number stamped or branded into the wood (or QR code as used at ETH Zurich). Record: species, section, treatment date, cut angles, node type at each end. This data allows future builders to reuse the components without re-engineering from scratch — the dome becomes a material bank, not a waste stream.

Foundation & Base Ring

Site preparation, 15 footing positions, frost depth, and drainage requirements for Chemnitz, Saxony.

Base node count — confirmed

15 four-way base perimeter hubs

A 3V 7/12 icosahedron dome has 15 four-way base perimeter hubs (confirmed from geo-dome.co.uk [R2]). This determines the foundation layout: 15 individual point footings arranged on the icosahedron base ring geometry — note this is not a regular polygon. Spacing between adjacent base nodes varies between approximately 2.0 m and 2.4 m depending on which strut type spans between them.

Frost depth — Chemnitz

Frost penetration: 80 cm in Saxony

DIN 1054 / local experience: frost penetration in Chemnitz can reach 80cm in severe winters. Foundation bases must extend to at least 90cm below finished ground level. Footing size: 400×400mm concrete pad, depth 900mm. Concrete grade: C20/25 minimum.

Base ring radius

Marking out the 15 base hubs

The base perimeter of a 3V 7/12 icosahedron is not a regular polygon. The exact X/Y coordinates of all 15 base nodes can be exported directly from Acidome as a plan view. Use a total station or laser level on-site to set out from these coordinates. Alternatively: use a 6m string from centre and place a nail at each Acidome-specified angle and distance.

01

Export base node coordinates from Acidome

Before any site work, get the plan-view coordinates of all 15 base nodes from Acidome. These are your survey control points — everything else is built from them.

02

Clear site, establish datum, excavate 15 pits

Remove topsoil across 13m diameter. Set datum with laser level. Excavate 450×450×950mm pits at all 15 node positions.

03

Pour footings with cast-in anchor bolts

2× M16 anchor bolts per footing, cast to match steel base plate hole pattern. Level all bolt tops to within 3mm — use a laser level across all 15 pads. C20/25 concrete, minimum 28 days cure before loading.

04

Install base plates and drainage gravel

Bolt 150×150×6mm steel base plates. Fill entire dome footprint with 200mm compacted clean angular gravel 10–20mm. This is the critical drainage layer preventing timber base node rot.

Phase 8

Assembly Sequence

Ground-up construction order. With struts now 2.0–2.5m long and 80×80mm timber, each strut weighs approximately 2.5–3.2 kg — manageable by one person but scaffold is essential.

Team

Minimum 4 people

Struts are physically large. You need 2 people at height on scaffold, 1 coordinating at ground, 1 supplying and documenting. Budget 3–5 full working days for frame assembly.

Scaffold

Hire Rollgerüst to 7m

Access to apex (~6.4m) is required. Hire a mobile aluminium scaffold tower rated to at least 7m working height for ~€90–120/week. Alternatively: erect a central temporary mast from a 7m timber post to support initial rows.

Pre-assembly

Pre-bolt all 60 triangles on ground

Assemble all 60 triangles flat on workshop floor before site. Number each triangle 1–60 per the Acidome assembly diagram. This dramatically reduces on-site complexity.

1

Install and verify 15 base plates

All 15 base plates level to ±3mm. Check diagonal distances between non-adjacent nodes to verify geometry before raising any timber.

2

Raise base ring triangles (Row 5 from apex)

The 15 base struts connect between base plate nodes. Stand and loosely bolt. Brace with temporary ropes until ring is geometrically stable. Do not tighten bolts yet.

3

Build rows 4, 3, 2 sequentially

Work ring by ring upward. The structure becomes self-supporting after row 4 is closed — geodesic triangulation locks itself. Use scaffold tower for rows 2 and above.

4

Close apex pentagon — most critical step

The apex 5-way node is the final closure. Have one person at the top holding the central rod while 5 beams are guided in simultaneously. Insert M12 rod and hand-tighten first. Verify all faces are flush before final torque.

5

Final-torque all nodes bottom to top

M12 nodes: 45 Nm. M16 base nodes: 85 Nm. Use calibrated torque wrench only. Mark each bolt with paint pen after torquing. Over-tightening splits timber.

6

Install polycarbonate panels bottom to top

Slide panels into routed grooves, seal with neutral-cure silicone, cap with aluminium U-channel. Leave ventilation panel positions open. Install door frame and vent hinges last.

Phase 10

Greenhouse Fit-Out

Internal layout, ventilation, irrigation, and climate control for a functional growing space.

Benches & layout

Circular path, radial beds

Central 1.2m path (gravel or timber decking) around a central column planter. Radial growing benches at 80cm height running from the central path to the perimeter. Hanging rails attached to lower-ring struts for climbers and trailing plants. Leave one base opening for the door unit (1 of the 15 base triangle positions).

Ventilation

Automatic bi-metal vent openers

Minimum 4 upper triangular panels as vents (apex ring) + 4 lower panels as air inlets. Bi-metal wax actuators open automatically at ~22°C, close on cooling (~€20 each). This provides passive stack ventilation without electricity. In summer: add external shading net over south-facing panels.

Irrigation

Drip system from single inlet

Run one 25mm PE water pipe through foundation at a base panel position. Install a 1,000L IBC tank outside dome for rainwater capture (gutter at base ring drains to tank). Drip lines from tank to each bench. Avoid overhead sprinklers — condensation on inner polycarbonate promotes mould at timber nodes.

Thermal performance

10mm twin-wall: U ≈ 3.0 W/m²K

The dome surface area of ~264m² with U=3.0 gives a heat loss of ~792 W per °C temperature difference. At −10°C outside / +15°C inside (ΔT=25°C), heat demand ≈ 19.8 kW. A 2kW fan heater provides approximately 10% of this demand — sufficient only to keep temperatures marginally above freezing (roughly −8°C to −7°C inside at −10°C outside), not to maintain growing temperatures. For active winter growing at +12–15°C, you would need heating capacity in the range of 15–20 kW, or significantly better insulation. For year-round growing, consider 16mm triple-wall polycarbonate (U≈2.0) on all panels, which would reduce heat demand to approximately 13.2 kW under the same conditions.

Maintenance & Lifespan

What to do and when — to achieve 25–40 year service life for the timber frame.

WhenTaskWhat to check / do
Every springRe-torque all node boltsTimber creeps and shrinks — expect 5–10% torque loss in year 1. Re-torque M12 to 45Nm, M16 to 85Nm using calibrated torque wrench.
Every springTimber inspectionLook for: grey surface (harmless weathering), black mould (treat with fungicide + re-oil), soft spots (rot — investigate and cut out). Pay most attention to base nodes and any areas near water pooling.
Every springRe-oil all exposed timberOne coat Leinöl-Holzöl exterior oil, all faces. Extra attention: joint cut faces, end grain, base node areas.
Every autumnSilicone sealant inspectionCheck all polycarbonate-to-timber seals. Replace any cracked or pulling silicone before winter rain season. Use neutral-cure silicone only.
Every autumnDrainage checkClear gravel of debris. Ensure water drains away from all 15 base nodes. No soil should be piling against timber.
Every 3 yearsBolt hole inspectionRemove one bolt per node type, inspect hole for moisture or decay. Reapply wood preservative to bolt hole interior if needed.
Every 5 yearsFull structural checkInspect all 15 base plates for rust. Check all 46 nodes for movement or cracking. Consider professional Sachverständiger inspection.
Every 8–12 yearsPolycarbonate replacementUV-stabilised 10mm twin-wall typically lasts 10 years before yellowing. Budget ~€1,800 for full re-cladding at current prices. Frame will outlast the cladding 3–4 times.
Expected lifespan

25–40 years — frame. 8–12 years — polycarbonate.

Properly treated Larch or Douglas Fir with annual oiling and well-drained base nodes will last 30–40 years. The Kruschke bolt system can be retorqued indefinitely. The main consumable is the polycarbonate. The ETH Zurich geodesic dome principle applies: each component is independently replaceable without demolishing the structure.
